Kit vs. a CRM for freelancers: which system do you need first?

Kit and a sales CRM both store information about people, but they manage different kinds of business relationships.

Fact-checked September 4, 2026

Editorial note: This guide is based on official product documentation and category-level analysis. It is not a hands-on performance test. No affiliate links are included.

Freelancers often hear that they need both an email marketing platform and a CRM. That can make a simple software decision feel more complicated—and more expensive—than it needs to be.

Kit is primarily an email marketing platform for building and nurturing an audience. A sales CRM is primarily a system for tracking leads, sales conversations, deals, and client follow-ups.

The short answer

  • Choose Kit first if your main problem is collecting email subscribers and staying in touch with them at scale.
  • Choose a CRM first if your main problem is keeping track of individual prospects, proposals, and next actions.
  • Consider both if content or email generates interest, but one-to-one conversations turn that interest into client work.

What Kit is designed to manage

Kit describes itself as an email marketing platform built for creators. Its core workflow centers on subscribers: people who join your email list through a form, landing page, or another signup source.

Kit can help a freelancer:

  • create landing pages and email signup forms;
  • send newsletters and one-time email broadcasts;
  • organize subscribers with tags and segments;
  • send automated email sequences;
  • sell digital products or subscriptions.

This makes Kit relevant to freelancers whose business depends partly on an audience. That could include consultants who publish educational content, coaches who use lead magnets, designers with a newsletter, or independent professionals who sell templates and courses alongside client services.

The key point is that Kit is designed around audience communication. It helps you collect permission to email people and send relevant messages based on their interests or actions.

What a sales CRM is designed to manage

A sales CRM is built around a different question:

What needs to happen next for this specific prospect or client?

Instead of focusing mainly on broadcasts and automated sequences, a CRM usually helps you track:

  • leads and contacts;
  • sales opportunities or deals;
  • pipeline stages;
  • calls, meetings, and notes;
  • proposals and follow-up dates;
  • the next action required to move a deal forward.

For example, a freelance consultant may have five potential clients at different stages. One has requested a proposal, one needs a follow-up call, two are still evaluating options, and one has stopped responding. A CRM gives those individual sales conversations a visible structure.

An email marketing platform may store all five email addresses, but that does not automatically make it the best place to manage the sales process.

Subscriber vs. lead: the practical difference

The same person can be both a subscriber and a lead, but the two labels describe different relationships.

Subscriber in Kit Lead in a CRM
Has joined an email audienceMay represent a potential sale
Organized by interests, signup source, or engagementOrganized by deal stage and next action
Receives newsletters, broadcasts, or sequencesReceives individual sales follow-up
Measured through engagement and conversionMeasured through pipeline progress and client outcomes

Imagine that someone downloads your free pricing checklist. At that point, the person is probably best treated as a subscriber. Kit can deliver the resource, tag the subscriber based on the signup form, and send a relevant welcome sequence.

If that person later requests a consultation, the relationship changes. You may now need to track the inquiry, discovery call, proposal, expected value, and follow-up date. That is where a CRM becomes more useful.

The exact boundary will differ by business. What matters is defining the moment when general audience interest becomes an individual sales opportunity.

Choose Kit first if your main problem is audience growth

Kit is likely to be the more useful first system when most of the following are true:

  • You attract potential clients through articles, social media, videos, a podcast, or a lead magnet.
  • You want to build an email list instead of relying entirely on social platforms.
  • You need a consistent way to welcome and educate new subscribers.
  • You want to send one message to many people while still grouping them by interest.
  • You do not yet have enough active sales conversations to require a formal pipeline.

For a freelancer at this stage, a CRM can become an empty database that adds administrative work without solving the immediate problem. If there are only one or two active inquiries at a time, a simple task list may still be enough for individual follow-up.

Kit can address the earlier part of the customer journey: turning anonymous visitors into subscribers and helping those subscribers understand your expertise over time.

That does not guarantee more leads or sales. The result will still depend on your offer, content, audience, signup incentive, and email quality. The software provides the system; it does not create demand by itself.

Choose a CRM first if your main problem is sales follow-up

A CRM is likely to be the better first system when most of the following are true:

  • Referrals and direct inquiries already generate most of your opportunities.
  • Your sales process involves discovery calls, proposals, and follow-up conversations.
  • Several prospects are at different stages at the same time.
  • You have missed follow-ups or forgotten what was agreed in a previous conversation.
  • You need to see the value and status of your potential work.
  • Your main bottleneck is closing and managing opportunities, not growing an audience.

In this situation, adding more subscribers may not address the operational problem. You may already have enough demand; you simply need a more reliable way to manage it.

A lightweight CRM can help a solo operator create a repeatable process without adopting a system designed for a large sales department. The right level of complexity depends on how many active opportunities you manage and how much information each sale requires.

When a freelancer may need both

Kit and a CRM can work as separate layers in the same business. A typical workflow might look like this:

  1. A visitor reads an article and joins your email list.
  2. Kit delivers the promised resource and sends a welcome sequence.
  3. The subscriber replies or submits a consultation form.
  4. The qualified inquiry is added to your CRM.
  5. The CRM tracks the discovery call, proposal, follow-up, and outcome.

In this model, Kit manages the one-to-many relationship before a sales conversation begins. The CRM manages the one-to-one relationship after the person becomes a genuine opportunity.

Using both can make sense when the handoff is clear. It makes less sense when the same information is copied between systems without a defined reason.

Before adding a second tool, decide:

  • What event turns a subscriber into a lead?
  • Which system is the source of truth for sales follow-up?
  • Which information actually needs to move between the two tools?
  • Can the process be handled manually until the volume justifies automation?

Do not assume you need an integration on day one. A manual handoff may be simpler and easier to audit while your process is still changing.

Where Kit may be a weaker fit

Kit may not be the best first choice for every freelancer. Consider another category of tool first if:

  • you do not plan to build or email an audience;
  • almost every prospect comes through private referrals;
  • you primarily need deal tracking and follow-up reminders;
  • you need detailed company, opportunity, or sales-pipeline records;
  • your email requirements are limited to individual client communication.

Kit’s tags and segments can organize subscribers, but subscriber organization is not the same as a dedicated deal pipeline. Trying to force every sales activity into an email platform can make important follow-ups harder to see.

The reverse is also true. A CRM may contain contact records and email features, but that does not necessarily make it the right tool for publishing a newsletter or running a creator-focused nurture system.

Bottom line

Choose Kit when your main problem is building and nurturing an audience. Choose a CRM when your main problem is tracking individual sales opportunities and client follow-ups.

If content generates your leads and personal conversations close them, you may eventually need both. But you do not need to implement both on day one.

Start with the system that solves the bottleneck you have now. Add the second system when the process—not the software marketing—shows that you need it.
